As NASA prepares for increasingly complex missions deeper into space, the role of human-robot collaboration continues to grow. At NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, the Dexterous Robotics Team works on robotic hardware and software designed to execute tasks typically performed by human hands, supporting future space exploration and human spaceflight.

## Building on a legacy of humanoid robotics

The 16-member team operates within NASA’s Robotic System Technology Branch. While organized into mechatronics and software subgroups, most team members possess cross-disciplinary experience in electronics, mechanics, and simulation software. Much of this expertise stems from past work on two well-known humanoid systems: Robonaut 2, which operated aboard the International Space Station for seven years, and Valkyrie, NASA’s first bipedal humanoid robot.

According to Dexterous Robotics Team lead Shaun Azimi, the group's objective is not to replace human explorers, but to make human exploration safer and more sustainable in extreme environments. Today, the team supports various agency projects tied to the development of the Moon Base, while also collaborating with external partners—including an oil and gas company—facing similar operational challenges in harsh environments.

## Testing technologies with iMETRO

A primary focus for the group is the Integrated Mobile Evaluation Testbed for Robotics Operations (iMETRO) facility at Johnson. The facility supports the adaptation of terrestrial robotic technologies for human-supervised space exploration applications such as logistics, maintenance, and scientific research.

iMETRO incorporates open-source software, simulation assets, space vehicle and habitat mockups, house robots, and an outdoor rock yard. This combination of digital and physical infrastructure allows users to test entire robots or individual hardware and software components.

The facility has hosted external collaborations, including tests by PickNik Inc. using software to help a robotic arm recognize a spacecraft hatch, turn the latch, grasp the handle, and open the door. Additionally, a NASA intern utilized the facility to test software using a commercial robotic arm and camera to inspect and maintain a cold stowage freezer similar to those on the space station.

While current technology development emphasizes supporting a sustained human presence on the lunar surface, Azimi noted that these innovations also apply to future missions to Mars. The team is collaborating with other agency organizations on an upcoming challenge inviting public ideas for Mars exploration technology solutions.
