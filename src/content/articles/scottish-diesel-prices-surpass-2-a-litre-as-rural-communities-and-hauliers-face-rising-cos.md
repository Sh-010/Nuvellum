Analysis of fuel pricing by BBC Scotland News has revealed that diesel prices have crossed the £2-per-litre threshold at least 226 forecourts across Scotland. The price spike is felt most severely in the country's rural and island communities, where fuel costs can be up to 50p higher per litre than at supermarkets in the central belt.

Prices in remote regions have climbed significantly, with some stations in the Hebrides charging as much as 238.8p and 223p per litre. In the Northern Isles, prices have reached up to 209p and 208.9p.

## Pressure on Hauliers and Rural Economies

The sharp increase in fuel costs has placed a massive financial burden on commercial transport operators. The Road Haulage Association (RHA) estimates that the price surge is costing businesses an additional £250 per week for every vehicle on the road.

For long-standing operators, the current climate is unprecedented. Norman MacAskill, who runs the South Uist-based N D MacAskill & Son, noted that when he entered the industry four decades ago, it cost just 32p a litre to fill a lorry. Now, paying £2.22 a litre, he describes the monthly fuel bills as devastating, warning that many established firms are going out of business.

In Orkney, Dave Neil of Sinclair Haulage reports spending £400 more each week on fuel than he did before the summer. To cope, his firm has introduced a temporary fuel surcharge for customers, which he hopes to remove if prices decline.

The impact is also rippling through agriculture and community services. The National Farmers' Union Scotland warned that rising fuel costs directly inflate food production expenses, particularly during the intensive autumn harvest. For local residents like Amy Henderson, a crofter and musician from Kiltarlity, the price increases require careful logistical planning regarding vehicle use and are affecting how she prices her musical performances.

In Inverness, the charity New Start Highland, which delivers donated furniture to families in need, reported that rising costs are putting direct pressure on both their operations and the vulnerable households they assist, accelerating their plans to transition to an electric fleet.

## Global Pressures and National Records

The price escalation is driven by rising global crude oil costs, fueled by conflicts in the Middle East and the Russia-Ukraine war. The RAC expects the average UK price of diesel to set a new record high, teetering near 198.32p. At this rate, filling a standard 55-litre family car costs approximately £109.

According to RAC policy head Simon Williams, the UK currently has the most expensive diesel in Europe, exceeding prices in Finland and the Netherlands by 12p per litre. Petrol has also hit its highest level in over four years, averaging 173.6p after rising nearly 12p in September and more than 40p since the war in Iran began. Diesel itself has risen by 14.5p in September alone, and by 55p since late February.

## Calls for Government Intervention

In response to the spiraling costs, industry bodies are urging the UK government to provide targeted relief. The RHA is lobbying for the cancellation of planned fuel duty increases in early next year and the introduction of an essential user rebate for commercial vehicles, similar to schemes already operating in Spain, France, and Italy.

The AA has also advocated for the retention of the current 5p fuel duty cut, which was first introduced in March 2022. The organization argues that while large corporations can pass fuel surcharges onto clients, smaller entities—such as independent trade workers, mobile vets, and taxi drivers—must absorb these costs directly.

The UK government has defended its position, pointing out that it extended the 5p fuel duty cut until December 31 and committed to no fuel duty increases this year. A spokesperson stated that this extension keeps diesel prices 11p per litre cheaper than they would have been under previous plans, helping shield motorists and businesses from the wider geopolitical crisis.
