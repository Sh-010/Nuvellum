Following the 2026 United States midterm elections, where Democrats secured control of both chambers of Congress, industry insiders and lawmakers are weighing the impact on efforts to pass a federal film and television production tax credit. The bipartisan Motion Picture, Television, and Entertainment Revitalization Act was introduced in September to establish a national incentive aimed at bringing production back to the United States.

Supporters originally hoped to attach the legislation to an end-of-year budget bill ahead of a December 11 vote. However, the sweeping election outcome has left the legislative timeline and strategy uncertain as lawmakers face shifting priorities.

## Divided industry views on election impact

Industry insiders remain split on whether the shift in congressional power threatens the legislation. One insider involved in the lobbying effort indicated that the political shift complicates the path to relief for Hollywood. Conversely, another source suggested that decisive election results prevent a protracted legislative battle and noted President Donald Trump's stated openness to bipartisan deals.

Film producer Scott Budnick expressed confidence that bipartisan backing remains strong, noting that high-ranking members from both parties previously collaborated on the measure and that the President had expressed support.

## Potential legislative scenarios

Lawmakers face multiple possible paths ahead of the new congressional session. Proponents still aim to attach the measure to an annual government funding bill to avoid a shutdown, relying on continued bipartisan support. Representative Laura Friedman emphasized the urgency of bringing jobs and investments back to the United States amid international competition.

However, the legislation could encounter roadblocks if it loses bipartisan backing. Several cosponsors lost their reelection bids, and the looming transition of power may prompt Republicans and Democrats to redirect their focus toward other legislative agendas, such as judicial nominations or broader economic issues. Once Democrats assume full control of Congress in January, they could theoretically pass the measure without Republican votes, though competing priorities like healthcare and oversight investigations could relegate the film tax credit to a lower position on the legislative agenda.
