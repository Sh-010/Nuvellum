Modern "romantasy" bestsellers—such as Sarah J Maas’s *A Court of Thorns and Roses* and Callie Hart’s *Quicksilver*—are frequently targeted by critics who view the genre as raunchy, badly written, and unashamedly escapist. However, according to author Sarah Clegg, these tales of human-sized fairies and intense romance are part of a centuries-old heritage originating in the French courts of the late 1100s.

## The medieval roots of fairy romance

Originating in the late 12th century, medieval romances spread rapidly across Europe and down social strata, reaching farm workers and impoverished nuns alike. Drawing on earlier myths and Arthurian legends, these stories provided escapism with glitter, blood, adventure, crystal palaces, and perilous enchanted realms. 

Like today's romantasy works, medieval romances featured wild sexual freedom, overflowing with sexually active fairies of both genders. Texts included descriptions of fairy bodies and detailed accounts of mortals and fairies taking what they desired, making merry, and having children together. Audiences also enjoyed brooding, threatening fairy characters that maintained their darker edges even in happy endings.

## Exploring desire and subverting gender roles

Because they were fictional, these historical stories served as a safe space to experiment with sex within repressed societies. Authored and consumed largely by women—such as 12th-century poet Marie de France—the romances allowed female audiences to explore desires free from traditional expectations of chastity. Concurrently, fairy sex was often viewed by medieval society as distinct from mortal relations, allowing women who engaged in it to retain their virginity in cultural perception.

The genre also inverted traditional gender roles. While modern romantasy features female heroes and military leaders alongside vulnerable male love interests, medieval narratives frequently depicted powerful fairy queens rescuing distressed knights, as seen in the 12th-century narrative poem *Lanval* by Marie de France.

## Historical criticism and endurance

Criticism of the genre is similarly enduring. Medieval commentators, including Chaucer in his romance parody *Sir Thopas*, mocked the reverence women held for romances. Today, modern romantasy authors face accusations of cheapening popular culture, a vitriol that analysts suggest stems largely from the genre being predominantly enjoyed by women. Despite centuries of critique, fairy romance continues to influence literature, leaving a legacy that stretches from Shakespeare to contemporary bestseller lists.
