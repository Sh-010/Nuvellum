Voters in Alberta are deciding whether the province should remain in Canada or begin the legal process toward a future referendum on separation. While historically a fringe cause, separatist sentiment grew following pandemic-era restrictions and the 2022 Freedom Convoy.

## Historical context and grievances

Alberta, which holds the world’s fourth-largest proven oil reserves and sends roughly 90% of its crude exports to the United States, has long experienced tensions with the federal government over energy policy and federal power. Policies enacted under Liberal prime ministers Pierre Trudeau and Justin Trudeau, including a national carbon tax and climate measures, drew significant opposition from local leaders who argued the regulations harmed the oil industry.

Former Alberta Premier Jason Kenney noted that separatist parties previously drew only 1% or 2% of the vote. However, pandemic-era restrictions and the 2022 Freedom Convoy—which began over vaccine requirements for cross-border truckers before broadening into a larger movement—helped mobilize activists. Jeff Rath, an independence movement leader, stated that federal vaccine rules acted as a turning point for many supporters.

## Political dynamics and international outreach

Alberta Premier Danielle Smith, who called the vote while supporting remaining in Canada, noted that polling regarding the separation process remained notable. Meanwhile, Prime Minister Mark Carney has gained popularity in the province due to business- and energy-friendly policies, including support for a proposed pipeline to the West Coast and opposition to U.S. trade policies.

Separatist advocates have also pursued international engagement, holding meetings with United States State Department officials to discuss potential energy infrastructure and the concept of an independent Alberta. A State Department spokesperson confirmed regular meetings with various representatives while emphasizing that the vote remains a decision for the people of Alberta. Kenney criticized the outreach as unauthorized discussions with a foreign government regarding a state that does not exist.

## Future outlook

Keith Wilson, an independence leader and former counsel for convoy organizers, described the vote as a mechanism to secure leverage in negotiations with Ottawa, even if full independence is not immediately attained. Kenney warned that even moderate support levels would embolden the movement, presenting a challenge for the conservative movement in the province.
