## The elusive definition of cinematography

Despite crafting memorable images for films ranging from *No Country for Old Men* to *Blade Runner 2049* and *1917*, veteran cinematographer Roger Deakins admits he still cannot answer a fundamental question about his profession. “Somebody asked the other day, what is a cinematographer? I mean, I still can’t answer that,” Deakins notes. He believes the work depends too heavily on the specific circumstances of a story and its storytellers to fit a single method, emphasizing that emotional reactions guide an image more than technical rules.

Reflecting on his beginnings in Torquay, England, Deakins recalls using the camera as a shield. “I was kind of hiding behind the camera to a certain degree,” he says. “I had confidence because there was something between me and reality.” 

## Collaboration and lived experience

That personal perspective expanded through experience and close collaboration with his wife and creative partner, James Ellis Deakins. The couple works together on every project and hosts the *Team Deakins* podcast. Ellis Deakins stresses that gathering diverse life experiences provides filmmakers with multiple points of view to enrich a narrative. Roger Deakins cautions against an inward-looking cinema where directors reference only past films rather than the real world.

Their collaborative approach extends to directors and actors. Harrison Ford previously noted that Deakins lights scenes from the story's perspective, making actors feel supported. Deakins expresses a preference for the intimacy of operating a camera directly beside a director rather than monitoring from afar, mourning the loss of that direct trust and collaboration.

## Instinct on set and beyond

Discussing specific choices, such as the evenly lit tent scene during Robert Ford’s murder in Andrew Dominik’s *The Assassination of Jesse James by the Coward Robert Ford*, Deakins acknowledges that practical set conditions and deliberate choices blend together. Whether working spontaneously with handheld cameras alongside Sam Mendes on *Jarhead* or planning meticulously with Joel and Ethan Coen, the primary focus remains on the audience's emotional placement within the story.

Outside of film sets, Deakins draws parallels between his lifelong hobby of fishing off the Devon coast and filmmaking. Both pursuits require patience, observation, and constant adaptation to changing conditions rather than adhering to a rigid plan.
