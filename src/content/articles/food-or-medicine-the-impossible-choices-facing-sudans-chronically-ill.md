Chronically ill residents across Sudan face difficult choices between purchasing essential medicines and covering basic necessities, such as food, as the country's civil war between the military and the Rapid Support Forces paramilitary group continues.

## Displacement and daily treatment struggles

The conflict, which began in April 2023, has displaced numerous citizens and disrupted access to consistent medical care. Hasanat al-Rashid, a 48-year-old diabetes patient, fled multiple locations—including Haj Yousif in Khartoum North and areas in Gezira State—before arriving in Port Sudan. During her displacement, she experienced periods without medication and consumed foods unsuitable for her condition due to availability issues.

Al-Rashid's doctor recommended an insulin pen, but at 70,000 Sudanese pounds ($8), the upfront cost is prohibitive. Instead, she purchases vials of insulin for 17,000 Sudanese pounds ($1.80). Lacking a refrigerator, she spends 180,000 Sudanese pounds ($20) monthly on ice to keep the medication cool. According to the United Nations Development Programme, the war has pushed income levels in Sudan back to those of the 1990s, compounding these financial pressures.

Similar challenges face other patients, such as 75-year-old Fatima Adam, who was admitted to Port Sudan Hospital for hypertension and thyroid complications. Before the war, Adam received subsidised medicine through the National Health Insurance Fund, paying only a quarter of the cost. Following the economic collapse, those medications are no longer covered by the scheme. Her family, reliant on day labor, often must buy smaller quantities of medicine to take intermittently when funds are low.

## Pharmaceutical supply strain

The National Medical Supplies Fund reported that the war has caused an estimated $500m in losses to medicine stocks, infrastructure, and transport fleets. The National Medicines and Poisons Board stated that only eight of the 30 pharmaceutical factories in Khartoum that closed during the conflict have resumed operations, heightening Sudan's reliance on imported medicine. This reliance, combined with the depreciation of the Sudanese pound—which has seen black-market rates reach 9,300 pounds per dollar compared to about 600 pounds before the war—has driven up procurement and production costs.

Pharmacists also report operational difficulties. Hossam al-Naeem, a pharmacist in Port Sudan, noted that rising supplier prices sometimes leave him with little to no profit margin after covering operating costs, making it difficult to keep essential medicines in stock.

In el-Obeid, Huda Mahmoud, 58, manages a heart condition requiring five different medicines. Following repeated displacements, she contends with monthly medication costs of 151,000 Sudanese pounds ($16.80). Her primary concern is running out of treatment, which carries the risk of blood clots.
