Director James Gray stated at the New York Film Festival premiere of his film *Paper Tiger* that he does not want to preach politics to audiences through his work. Speaking at Alice Tully Hall, Gray emphasized his preference for movies driven by subtext rather than direct lessons. 

“All movies are political in one way or another, but I like movies that exist with subtext,” Gray told *Variety*. “I don’t want to preach to you. That’s a polemic. That’s for debate class... The reason we can watch some of those beautiful Hollywood movies from 1940 and still find them moving is because they weren’t trying to teach us a lesson. Not Sunday school. Art is not Sunday school.”

## Unexpected political parallels

Despite Gray's stance on avoiding direct political messaging, *Paper Tiger* has encountered unintentional political parallels regarding its title. Historically used to describe something seemingly powerful that poses little threat, the term "paper tiger" gained prominent modern usage when President Donald J. Trump invoked it regarding Vladimir Putin’s invasion of Ukraine.

Gray noted that he wrote and made the film before Trump began using the term in that context. “It had nothing to do with him…. It’s sort of like half frightening, weirdly flattering. I don’t know. What would you think?” Gray said. When asked if Trump himself is a paper tiger following announcements about trilateral peace talks, Gray declined to comment.

## Inside *Paper Tiger*

Starring Adam Driver, Scarlett Johansson, and Miles Teller, *Paper Tiger* is a crime drama centered on familial and financial peril. Driver plays Gary, a former police officer who involves his younger brother Irwin (Teller) in a risky business deal that entangles them with the Russian mafia. The conflict disrupts Irwin's suburban family life alongside his wife Hester, played by Johansson.

Gray's filmography frequently draws from personal history, following earlier works like *The Yards* and the autobiographical *Armageddon Time*. While *Paper Tiger* was initially conceived as a sequel exploring his mother's battle with glioblastoma, the narrative ultimately shifted to focus on themes reminiscent of his father's legal entanglements.

Reflecting on the title, Gray highlighted its deeper thematic resonance regarding American vulnerability. “This term was originally [popularized] by Mao Zedong in China to describe us,” Gray said. “I always loved it because it seemed so vivid. A tiger made of paper — what is that? It’s the brittleness that’s underneath all of us no matter how tough we think we are.”
