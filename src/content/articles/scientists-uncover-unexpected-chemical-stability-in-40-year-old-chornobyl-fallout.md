Forty years after the Chornobyl nuclear disaster, microscopic radioactive particles measuring between 8 and 50 micrometers remain scattered across surrounding soil, continuing to pose health hazards that require protective clothing to navigate. Researchers studying these remnants have discovered that some particles have maintained much of their original internal structure despite decades of environmental exposure.

## analyzing radioactive particles in unprecedented detail

To understand the long-term behavior of the fallout, scientists isolated six particles from Ukrainian soil samples and transported them under heavy containment to the Rossendorf Beamline in Grenoble, France. Researchers used synchrotron X-ray diffraction, rotating the particles in an X-ray beam focused to about the thickness of a human hair and measuring each fragment from 2,000 distinct angles. 

This process allowed crystallographers and physicists to distinguish different uranium oxide phases within the debris. The analysis revealed three distinct classes of particles: those chemically similar to uranium dioxide fuel, particles partially encased in or fused with a zirconium layer from the reactor's protective cladding, and fragile uranium oxides created when the graphite moderator burned for ten days during the accident.

## unexpected stability and future risks

The findings showed that despite spending four decades exposed to the elements, the nuclear fuel inside several of the examined fragments retained much of its original crystal structure. This chemical stability suggests that certain particles may trap fission products within their structures rather than rapidly releasing them into the environment, potentially limiting the movement of contaminants into nearby soil and water.

However, researchers cautioned that the findings do not imply the area is becoming harmless. Because the study only examined six particles from two locations, the results cannot establish universal behavior across the entire contaminated zone. Individual particles vary significantly in composition and resistance to weathering, meaning persistent outliers will continue releasing radionuclides over extended periods. Consequently, scientists emphasize that current restrictions on the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone cannot be lifted anytime soon.
