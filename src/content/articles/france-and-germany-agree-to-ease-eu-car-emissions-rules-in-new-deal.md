France and Germany have backed looser European Union emissions rules for carmakers and agreed to include Britain in a "made in Europe" scheme. The provisional deal, reached on Thursday, remains subject to a final sign-off from Berlin.

## Revisions to emission targets and requirements

Under the provisional accord, the two economic powerhouses agreed to scale down emission rules for vehicles. The European Commission had previously proposed that a planned ban on new petrol and diesel vehicle sales in 2035 be replaced by a requirement for manufacturers to cut exhaust emissions by 90 percent, with the remaining 10 percent compensated through credits such as low-carbon steel usage.

The Franco-German pact removes the requirement for manufacturers to compensate for residual emissions. It also allows cars running on renewable fuels to count as "zero-emission," simplifies meeting CO2 targets for automakers, and extends "super credits" originally proposed for small electric cars to all vehicles regardless of size.

## The 'made in Europe' scheme and Britain

Negotiators also reached a compromise regarding public procurement rules intended to support manufacturing inside the bloc. Germany had sought a broad interpretation of "made in Europe" to include key non-EU trade partners, while France pushed to keep the scheme restricted to EU nations. 

The resulting draft restricts the definition to the EU's 27 member states while allowing an opt-in for a select few countries, notably Britain and Norway, under specific conditions. European carmakers had advocated for Britain's inclusion due to deeply integrated supply chains.

The office of Prime Minister Andy Burnham welcomed the move, stating that inclusion would benefit the British automotive industry as well as the jobs and investment it supports across the United Kingdom.
