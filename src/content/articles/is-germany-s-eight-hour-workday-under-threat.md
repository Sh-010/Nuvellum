## The debate over German working hours

Under current German law, standard working hours are generally restricted to eight hours per day, though this can be extended to ten hours if workers receive compensatory time off and maintain a minimum of 11 hours of rest between shifts. Business associations and the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and Christian Social Union (CSU) parties have long advocated for greater flexibility, citing the European Working Time Directive, which permits a maximum weekly working time of 48 hours.

Proponents of reform argue that existing regulations are overly rigid for industries with seasonal fluctuations, project-based work, or irregular peak periods. Chancellor Friedrich Merz expressed support for relaxing the rules following a visit to a biorefinery in Leuna, stating that working beyond eight hours can offer flexibility for both businesses and employees.

## Coalition divisions and union resistance

While the CDU/CSU and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) agreed in their 2025 coalition agreement to pursue reforms allowing for maximum weekly rather than daily working limits, the parties remain divided over implementation. Trade unions and the SPD have raised concerns regarding employee health and potential pressures on workers with family and caregiving responsibilities.

A draft reform introduced in June 2026 by the Labor and Social Affairs Ministry—then headed by SPD politician Bärbel Bas—included conditions such as collective bargaining agreements, health and safety regulations, and electronic tracking of working hours. The draft faced sharp criticism and rejection from business groups and the CDU, leaving the reform stalled.

## Political standoff and future outlook

The dispute centers on the balance between state-mandated worker protections and labor market flexibility. The SPD's labor working group released a subsequent paper emphasizing joint decision-making between employers and employees, further highlighting ongoing political tensions within the government over the future of the nation's labor regulations.
