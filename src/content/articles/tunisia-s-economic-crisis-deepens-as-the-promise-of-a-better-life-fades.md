## Mounting economic pressures and unemployment

Tunisia's economic difficulties have left many university graduates struggling to find work, turning joblessness into a symbol of wider financial pressure and frustration with successive governments. Cherine Ghanmi, a university graduate from the central Sidi Bou Zid governorate who finished her studies in 2012, remains unemployed more than a decade later. Instead of providing financial support to her family as she had hoped, she continues to rely on them.

According to Tunisia's National Institute of Statistics (INS), the overall unemployment rate stood at 14.9 percent in the second quarter of 2026. Among university graduates, the rate reached 26.6 percent, with women graduates facing an unemployment rate of 35.6 percent compared with 14.2 percent for men. For young people aged 15 to 24, unemployment reached 35.4 percent.

Economic growth figures remain low, with the INS reporting growth of 2.7 percent in 2025 and 2.4 percent in the first half of 2026. In September, the World Bank projected growth of 2.3 percent for the full year. Anis Ben Said, a tax adviser and university professor, noted that the country's core issue is an inability to turn skills into production, investment, and jobs, compounded by poor infrastructure and unreliable public services.

## The 'Dignity March' and public-sector recruitment laws

On September 27, unemployed university graduates initiated a roughly 300km (186-mile) march from Menzel Bouzaiane toward Tunis. Known as the "Dignity March," the demonstration began with 23 participants and grew to more than 120, according to coordinating committee estimates. The marchers aimed to demand jobs and the implementation of legislation intended to facilitate public-sector employment.

On October 6, police blocked the group in Borj Cedria, south of Tunis, telling participants they could travel individually rather than as a group. The marchers had sought to reach the presidential palace in Carthage.

Graduates have sought the implementation of official recruitment pathways. While 2022 Law No. 38 stalled amid financial constraints, 2025 Law No. 18 provided for the exceptional recruitment of long-term unemployed graduates into the public sector over a period not exceeding three years. However, marchers and representatives have demanded a clear timetable and practical arrangements, expressing skepticism over unfulfilled promises.

## Broader discontent and political friction

On October 17, thousands gathered in Tunis for one of the largest demonstrations against President Kais Saied in years. Led by the Tunisian General Labour Union (UGTT), opposition groups, and civil society organizations, protesters voiced frustration over unemployment, restrictions on freedoms, and difficulties in meeting basic needs.

President Saied, who has concentrated power since 2021, has defended his political project as a response to corruption and previous political failures. Critics argue that this concentration of power has weakened democratic checks and balances without resolving the economic crisis.

Public frustration has also been fueled by disruptions to basic services. During the summer, extreme heat coincided with electricity cuts and water shortages. Reuters reported that these recurring cuts were among the grievances raised at the October 17 demonstration, affecting local businesses and households.

Faced with ongoing economic strain, some younger Tunisians expressed intentions to leave the country. In a northern suburb of Tunis, 20-year-old barber Khalil stated he was waiting for an opportunity to depart by boat, noting that his generation has borne the brunt of consecutive crises.
