Fighting has broken out in Ethiopia's northern region of Tigray between regional forces and the national army. The clashes follow more than six months of deteriorating relations between the federal government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF), the party governing Tigray. The renewed hostilities threaten to undo the Pretoria Agreement, an African Union-brokered peace deal that ended a brutal two-year war in November 2022.

Residents in Tigray, who have been attempting to rebuild after a conflict that an African Union mediator estimated caused some 600,000 deaths and pushed the region toward famine, are facing renewed panic buying, internet disruptions, and cash shortages. Major clashes that erupted in Tigray have quickly spread into the neighbouring Afar and Amhara regions.

## Unresolved Issues and Faltering Peace

The collapse of the truce stems from several unresolved issues. Foremost among them is the disputed territory of western Tigray, which was captured during the civil war by Amhara fighters allied with the Ethiopian army. Hundreds of thousands of ethnic Tigrayans fled the territory and remain displaced. The TPLF demands the return of western Tigray and its displaced population, while the federal government maintains that a referendum must decide the area's future.

Disarmament has also been a major point of contention. The government asserts that the TPLF has retained its arms and handed over only old weaponry to the African Union. Additionally, disputes persist over the governance of Tigray. Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed appointed an interim administration under the peace deal, but internal disputes and the TPLF's decision to restore its pre-war administration ahead of a general election led to suspended civil servant pay and seizing public services.

Both sides assign blame for the latest escalation. The TPLF states it acted in response to a federal offensive involving drone strikes, whereas the government claims Tigrayan forces attacked first after seizing control of local airports.

## Shifting Alliances and Regional Pressures

Politically, the TPLF has moved away from its previous isolation by joining six other armed movements in September to form the Alliance for Survival. This new coalition includes the Amhara Fano militia and the Oromo Liberation Army. Although members hold distinct grievances, they share the objective of removing Prime Minister Abiy's administration from power. Analysts caution that maintaining this alliance could prove difficult given the conflicting interests of its members.

The federal government has criticized the coalition as an effort to destabilize the nation. Concurrently, regional tensions have frayed relations between Ethiopia and neighbouring Eritrea. Prime Minister Abiy has recently pushed for ownership of Eritrea's southern port of Assab and hinted at potential military action to secure access to the Red Sea, a move that compounds regional instability.

## International Response and Outlook

Diplomatic efforts to halt the violence are underway. The African Union has called for an immediate cessation of hostilities and urged a return to direct talks, deploying former Nigerian President Olusegun Obasanjo as its special Horn of Africa envoy. The United Nations and the regional organization Igad have issued similar appeals, warning against a wider and more destructive conflict.

With trust between Addis Ababa and Tigray at low levels, and armed conflicts continuing simultaneously in regions like Amhara and Oromia, international observers remain concerned that the fighting could engulf the broader region.
