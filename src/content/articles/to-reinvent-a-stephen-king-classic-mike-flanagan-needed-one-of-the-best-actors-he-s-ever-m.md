## Redefining Margaret White

For his modern reimagining of the Stephen King classic *Carrie* on Prime Video, showrunner Mike Flanagan built the series around a fundamental structural change: reimagining the relationship between Carrie White and her mother, Margaret. Rather than portraying a purely horrific home life as seen in Brian De Palma's 1976 film, Flanagan wanted a foundation of real love between mother and daughter, even if that dynamic remains colored by fear and emotional abuse.

Actor Samantha Sloyan steps into the role of Margaret White, bringing together elements from her previous collaborations with Flanagan, including her standout performances in *Midnight Mass*, *The Midnight Club*, and *The Haunting of Hill House*. Flanagan praised Sloyan's talent, describing her as "one of the best actors I've ever met."

## Updating the story for modern society

Flanagan's adaptation updates the original narrative by altering how Carrie is bullied to reflect contemporary society. In the series, Carrie's tormentor, Chris Hargensen, creates an anonymous Instagram account called Carrie Red to spread rumors. In response, Margaret embraces social media, establishing her own account and launching a movement to expose the student behind the harassment.

While Margaret succeeds in bringing the bully to justice, she becomes enamored with internet attention and pushes an anti-bullying movement past its limits until it takes on a violent edge. Sloyan notes that Margaret is just as susceptible to the charms of the internet as anyone else, driven by a misguided and frightening love for her daughter.

## Approaching complex villains

Sloyan is well known for playing antagonists across Flanagan's projects, most notably the religious fundamentalist Bev Keane in *Midnight Mass*. According to Flanagan, Sloyan's effectiveness as a villain stems from her profound empathy and egoless approach to acting. Rather than viewing characters like Bev Keane or Margaret White simply as villains, Sloyan approaches them by seeing the world entirely through their eyes, portraying characters who believe they are acting as heroes or victims in their own minds.
