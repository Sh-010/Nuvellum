After a six-year closure for vital restoration work, the historic Palm Houses at the Royal Botanic Garden in Edinburgh have reopened to the public.

## A major botanical operation

The closure became necessary after the 19th-century buildings grew leaky and rusty, requiring a complete overhaul. During the project, more than 5,000 panes of glass were replaced, corroding ironwork was repaired and repainted, stonework was renovated, and a partition wall between the two houses was removed to create a lighter, more open space.

Hundreds of plants had to be temporarily moved into pots and interim homes during the repairs. Glass house curator Sadie Barber noted that moving mature plants carried significant stress and risk, but the vast majority survived the process and are already showing signs of new growth.

## Protecting rare and endangered species

Prof Julia Knights, Regius Keeper of the Botanic Garden, emphasized the importance of the collection, which includes palms, conifers, ferns, and begonias. Many of the preserved species are endangered or extinct in the wild and hold potential medicinal properties for human health.

While the vast majority of the collection was successfully saved and returned, the restoration suffered one major casualty: a Sabal palm tree over 200 years old. Because it had grown too tall for the structure and could not be safely transplanted or stored, it had to be cut down. However, staff preserved seedlings from the tree, and one of its descendants has been planted in its place within the taller renovated glass house.

David Knott, director of horticulture, expressed hope that visitors from Scotland and around the world will once again enjoy the iconic space as the restoration guarantees the future of the structures.
