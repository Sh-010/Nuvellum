## Expanding the golden visa model

Traditionally, golden visa programs offered residency to wealthy foreigners in exchange for investments in real estate or businesses. The United Arab Emirates broadened this approach when it launched its golden visa system in 2019, providing 10-year residency not only to individuals investing at least $545,000 in property, but also to researchers, teachers, and specialized talent.

The program expanded further in subsequent years to include doctors, nurses, engineers, technology workers, and e-sports gamers. According to Kristin Surak, a professor of political sociology at the London School of Economics, the UAE adapted a status typically reserved for wealthy individuals and created an umbrella program blending labor, industrial policies, and capital attraction.

“It’s not a traditional golden visa,” Surak told *Fortune*. “Gold sounds cooler. You want a lead scientist? You call [the visa] gold.”

## Targeted sector growth and independence

Dubai has utilized the program to support specific industries. The city has issued golden visas to more than 1,400 nurses and midwives, as well as over 223 educators from private schools, universities, and early childhood centers. Long-term residency is also part of a wider initiative to expand the gaming sector by 2033, aiming to create 30,000 gaming jobs and contribute around $1 billion to GDP, alongside supporting a growing base of over 350 gaming companies.

For workers such as Swanand Pagnis, co-founder of enterprise AI engineering firm Superlinear Insights, the main advantage of the golden visa is independence from an employer-sponsored status. Previously, foreigners relied on two-year, employment-linked visas that tied their legal right to remain in the country directly to their jobs. The 10-year residency allows individuals to sponsor their families independently.

## Geopolitical shifts and population resilience

While the UAE does not levy income taxes, it faces shifting sentiment following regional conflicts. After Iran launched missiles and drones at Gulf states, including the UAE, during its conflict with the United States, Dubai experienced a temporary population dip. Official figures cited by *The National* showed Dubai lost 61,000 residents in March, though the population later rebounded to 4.74 million by the end of June.

Immigration experts noted a moderation in inquiries and applications due to geopolitical uncertainty. However, the UAE government introduced relief packages, including faster issuance and renewal of residency permits, alongside measures to assist residents stranded by airspace closures and flight disruptions, without publicly changing the golden visa's eligibility rules.
