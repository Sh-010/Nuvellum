On October 3, voters in Latvia will elect a new 100-seat parliament, the Saeima, with security and the economic impact of distancing from Moscow dominating the campaign agenda. Polls indicate the centrist United List, led by Prime Minister Andris Kulbergs, is currently ahead, followed by the populist alliance Sovereign Power/Young Latvians Alliance and Latvia First led by Ainars Slesers.

## Security concerns and NATO alignment

Security has emerged as a central issue in the election, with defense promises featured in the programs of most leading parties. Recent months have seen heightened regional tensions, including drone incidents in Baltic airspace—such as a September 15 incident in Lithuania involving a drone believed to have crossed from Belarus—and hybrid threats including migrant flows that Latvia and its neighbors report are encouraged by Belarus to pressure the European Union and test border security. Intelligence officials from several European countries, including Latvia and the Czech Republic, have warned that Russia could test NATO in the coming months.

In response to these security concerns, Germany deployed four Air Force Eurofighter jets to Latvia's Lielvarde military air base to strengthen Baltic air defenses. Latvia remains one of the few NATO members legally committed to spending 5% of GDP on defense. Analysts note that the country's foreign policy orientation is unlikely to shift significantly regardless of the election outcome, given nearly unanimous cross-party consensus on the importance of NATO and positive public views of the alliance.

## Economic costs and trade debates

While defense policy enjoys broad agreement, the economic fallout of cutting ties with Russia remains a point of contention. Prior to the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Russia was a key trading partner for Latvia. Since 2022, Latvia has reduced imports from Russia and Belarus by over 90% and introduced strict trade restrictions. While the broader economy has adapted, pre-war trading links and investments have been heavily affected.

While roughly 60% of Latvians favor reducing dependence on Russia according to the FES Security Radar 2025, some political figures advocate for a more pragmatic economic approach. Parties such as Sovereign Power/Young Latvians Alliance and Latvia First have argued against completely severing economic ties or halting goods transit not explicitly barred by EU sanctions, emphasizing growth and business interests. However, party representatives acknowledge that fully restoring trade while the war in Ukraine continues is not feasible.

## Language, identity, and voting uncertainty

Analysts suggest that pragmatic appeals from alternative parties may resonate with portions of Latvia's Russian-speaking minority—comprising about one-third of the population—who feel alienated by mainstream policies, including the phase-out of Russian-language education and a reduced public role for the language.

Political analyst Filips Rajevskis noted that predicting the election outcome is difficult, as many voters remain hesitant to share their voting preferences or intentions ahead of the ballot.
