## From the Tokyo Olympics to bedbound life

Five years after representing Great Britain at the Tokyo Olympics, 32-year-old sprinter Beth Dobbin spends more than 22 hours a day in bed. Dobbin has been diagnosed with myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME), a chronic, fluctuating illness that causes severe fatigue, pain, sleep problems, and cognitive difficulties. 

Her health issues began in the winter of 2021-22 following back-to-back Covid and glandular fever infections, which she initially contracted without acute illness. Despite experiencing worsening symptoms—including soreness, dizziness, and heavy fatigue—she competed in an unusually condensed season that included the World Championships, Commonwealth Games, and European Championships. Following a major rest period, her symptoms escalated to the point where she struggled to stand and was sleeping constantly.

## Rejection of the chronic fatigue label

Dobbin strongly rejects the alternative label of chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS). Having balanced an eight-hour shift schedule with athletic training prior to turning professional, she states she has known fatigue her entire life and feels the term fails to reflect the severity of her condition. 

She describes the illness as feeling like her entire body is shutting down, noting that simple stimuli like bright lights or loud sounds can trigger crashes lasting months. This year, she has left her family home in Doncaster on only five occasions.

## Raising awareness and future outlook

Dobbin acknowledges that speaking out about her condition carries personal health risks, as even a 30-minute conversation can trigger a severe physical reaction. However, she feels a duty to use her platform as an athlete to raise awareness for an illness that lacks a known cure or specific treatment.

Her family, including her parents Jim and Jean and her sister Faye, highlight the lack of specialist services and support within the National Health Service. To support research, Faye is running the Yorkshire Marathon to raise funds for ME Research UK. While Dobbin describes her future outlook as bleak, she expresses hope that increased attention and research stemming from post-viral conditions following the pandemic may eventually lead to medical advancements.
